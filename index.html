Article 1

Mr Maqabunga: The Scent of Ambition

Khayelihle Xolani Mathebula is a third-year IT student who never expected a compliment to turn into a business. It started when friends, strangers and almost everyone he bumbed into kept telling him he smelled incredible that whatever cologne he wore should be bottled and sold. Eventually, he took the hint. With just R800 in his pocket, he bought his first stock of perfumes and colognes and set a price point every student could afford, no matter what their gender.

The name came naturally. His friends started calling him "Mr Maqabunga” a direct translation of "Mr Scent” and it stuck so well that it became his brand identity before he even had a logo. He didn't need to hustle for his first customers either; his friends did the advertising for him, posting about his scents on social media until orders started rolling in on their own.

Running a fragrance business on a campus full of competitors wasn't without its challenges. Price wars were constant, with other sellers undercutting him to lure customers away. But Khayelihle held onto his loyal base by extending credit to regulars he trusted. That was a move that built long-term relationships instead of one-off sales. To keep his studies from suffering, he doesn't sell on demand; he sets scheduled selling times and sticks to them, protecting his degree while keeping "Mr Maqabunga" alive.

Looking ahead, Khayelihle's real goal is to become a software engineer. But he's not planning to let the perfume business go, he intends to keep it running alongside his tech career, proof that a side hustle doesn't have to stay small or temporary.

Nailed by Tasha: A Legacy in Her Hands

For Natasha Tsotsotso, nails were never just a hobby, they were a family inheritance. Her mother was a nail technician, and Natasha grew up watching her work. When her mom landed a demanding new job in Grade 11 and could no longer keep up with her clients, Natasha stepped in and took over the business herself. Her mother didn't just hand it over, though she invested in her daughter's growth, paying for lessons so Natasha could refine and professionalize her skill.

The transition to university wasn't instant success. Natasha struggled at first to find clients in an unfamiliar environment, far from the loyal customer base her mother had built at home. But persistence paid off, and today she's known as the go-to, trusted nail technician on campus. Now a fourth-year Education student, she protects her academic workload fiercely only taking clients on weekends and during holidays, never letting the business bleed into study time.

Her long-term vision goes beyond nails altogether. Natasha dreams of becoming a high school teacher, and she's already planning how to merge her two passions: she wants to offer free or discounted nail services to matric farewell students who can't afford to get glammed up for their big night turning her hustle into a way of giving back.

She says, “If you do it with love, you will do it even when it feels hard.” Natasha takes this as a lifetime gift has mother gave her and she is very thankful for that.

BarbieMaker: Painting Faces Like Canvases

Asthandile Nkosi has always been an artist long before makeup entered the picture. She used to draw evrything and everywhere. A 22-year-old Studio Arts and Design student, Asthandile didn't have one lightning-bolt moment that pulled her into makeup artistry; it was simply a natural extension of how she already saw the world. Faces, she realized, were just another canvas.

She started her business modestly, saving R500 over three months from her NSFAS allowance to buy her first kit. Unlike some hustles that take off quickly, finding clients has been a consistent uphill climb for Asthandile and remains one of her biggest challenges to this day. Her business has a rhythm, though: it peaks around graduation season, in March and September, when students want to look their best for the stage.

Because her hustle isn't an everyday operation, Asthandile finds it easy to balance with her studies the seasonal nature of the work means it rarely competes with deadlines. Interestingly, despite her talent and the demand around graduations, makeup isn't part of her long-term plans. For Asthandile, it's a skill she's built and enjoys, not the career she's chasing.

Candy T Weaves, and No Fixed Budget

Candy Thompho, a fourth-year Computer Science student, noticed a gap that a lot of students complain about, but few do anything about: stylists' prices for weave installs were simply too high for the average student budget. So, she taught herself the craft practicing first on her own hair before she ever touched a client's and built an affordable alternative from scratch.

Candy's approach to capital was different from most: instead of investing a lump sum upfront, she buys her stock in pieces from Takealot and Shein as orders come in, meaning she never had one fixed starting amount. It's a lean, low-risk way of running a hustle that trends have made even easier weave installs are popular right now, and her clients mostly come through residence mates and word-of-mouth compliments.

Because each install only takes twenty minutes to an hour, fitting the business around lectures and study sessions has been simple, especially since she lives on campus. But Candy is clear-eyed about what this is: fun, flexible extra income, not a long-term business she's building toward. Her real future is in tech, as she's aiming for a corporate career in coding, and the weave installs will stay exactly what they are now: a side hustle.

Article 5

The Walker: Stitching a Global Dream

Sphoesihle Manci's brand, The Walker, began the way a lot of teenage hustles do with a bit of community pressure and a trend he didn't want to miss out on. He started in Grade 10, offering personalized phone covers, embroidered T-shirts, and embroidered hats. Luckily, he had an advantage most beginners don't: his sister sews. That meant his only real startup cost was around R800 for embroidery work, before he eventually invested in his own equipment.

The early days weren't glamorous. Back home, competition was fierce and the business ran at a loss often. It wasn't until he got to university now studying Public Management in his first year, with plans to transfer into Arts and Design next year that The Walker really found its footing. Personalized phone covers have become his best-selling product by far.

Balancing a growing brand with first-year academic pressure hasn't been easy; Sphoesihle admits he missed deadlines and classes while trying to keep both going. But his ambition hasn't dimmed his dream is for The Walker to become a globally recognized brand, and it's that vision that's pushing him to switch his studies toward Arts and Design, aligning his degree with the creative direction he wants his business to take.
